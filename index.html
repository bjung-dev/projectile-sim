Mathematical models


      

        
The three kinematic equations

        
Every number in this simulator comes from these three equations for motion with
          constant acceleration \(a\) along a straight line:

        

          
(1)\[v = v_0 + a t\]

          
(2)\[\Delta x = v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2} a t^2\]

          
(3)\[v^2 = v_0^2 + 2 a \Delta x\]

        

        
Here \(v_0\) is the initial velocity, \(v\) the velocity after time \(t\), and \(\Delta x\) the displacement.

        
Assumptions: no air resistance, constant gravity \(g\), flat ground, and the projectile is treated as a point.

      


      

        
Setting up the problem

        
The key idea is that horizontal and vertical motion are independent. We apply the
          equations to each axis separately. Splitting the launch velocity into components:

        \[v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta \qquad v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta\]
        

          

            
Horizontal (x)Vertical (y)

            
              
Initial velocity\(v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta\)\(v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta\)

              
Acceleration\(a_x = 0\)\(a_y = -g\)

              
Start position\(x = 0\)\(y = h\)

            
          

        

        
Because \(a_x = 0\), equation (1) says \(v_x = v_{0x}\) stays constant for the whole flight.

      


      

        
Maximum height

        
At the top of its arc the projectile stops rising for an instant, so \(v_y = 0\).
          Apply equation (3) vertically, with \(a = -g\):

        \[0 = v_{0y}^2 - 2g\,\Delta y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta y = \frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}\]
        
\[y_{max} = h + \frac{(v_0\sin\theta)^2}{2g}\]

        
The time it takes to get there follows from equation (1) with \(v_y = 0\):

        \[0 = v_{0y} - g\,t_{peak} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{peak} = \frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}\]
      


      

        
Total flight time

        
The flight ends when the projectile hits the ground, which is a vertical displacement of
          \(\Delta y = -h\). Apply equation (2) vertically:

        \[-h = v_{0y}\,t - \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 - v_{0y}\,t - h = 0\]
        
This is a quadratic in \(t\). The quadratic formula gives one positive root:

        
\[t_{flight} = \frac{v_0\sin\theta + \sqrt{(v_0\sin\theta)^2 + 2gh}}{g}\]

        
Special case, launched from the ground (\(h = 0\)):
          \(t_{flight} = \dfrac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g} = 2\,t_{peak}\). The way up and the way down take equal time.

      


      

        
Range

        
Horizontally there is no acceleration, so equation (2) with \(a_x = 0\) reduces to
          distance = velocity × time. Use the flight time found above:

        
\[R = v_{0x}\,t_{flight} = v_0\cos\theta \cdot t_{flight}\]

        
Special case, \(h = 0\): substitute \(t_{flight} = 2v_0\sin\theta / g\) and use
          \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta\):

        \[R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}\]
        
\(\sin 2\theta\) is largest when \(2\theta = 90^\circ\), so from level ground the maximum range is at
          θ = 45°. Complementary angles such as 30° and 60° give the same range. From a raised launch
          point (\(h > 0\)) the best angle is a little below 45°. Try it in the simulator above.
